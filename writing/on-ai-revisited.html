On AI, revisited

I’m still a sceptic, and it’s still a tool. But it’s a tool that multiplies what people can do, and the best thing it does is take the grind out of work so we can get back to the human bits.

Still a sceptic

I still hold my scepticism, and I still ask what all of this means for the planet. And yes, it’s a tool. But it’s a tool that empowers people. It’s a force multiplier, and something you can engage with to craft some genuinely compelling things.

Vague in, vague out

If you plug something vague and generic into AI, you’ll get something vague and generic out. That’s the same as everything.

Give it proper context. Build robust skills that it can use. Give it clear instructions about what to output, in what format and in what context. Do that, and you get to a really robust place.

Taking out the grind

It’s extremely good at removing monotony. We’ve found that. It’s good at pattern recognition, and it’s good at taking work that is effectively grind and optimising it.

It’s got to the point where I now teach other people how to use AI, right down to using SharePoint agents to dig through folders and find things. There’s so much more to it than a large language model spitting out data. It can help you find things and do things effectively, and put the people back into the human part of work.

We’ve been acting as computers

For a long time now, we’ve done jobs that belong to computers. We’ve done them by hand and called it work. We’ve called it meaning.

We should be focusing on the things that are human to human: empathy, feeling and emotion. The things a computer can never do.

Not monotonous tasks that, really, anyone could be taught to do. The tangible things, the soft skills, the work that needs people interacting with people.

What it can do, and what it can’t

We can teach a large language model to sift through a transcript, spot themes and turn them into a thematic analysis. That’s not a problem.

What we can’t do is teach it to sit with someone in an interview and build a genuine connection, the kind that beats the uncanny valley in user research. It can’t design with the same emotion as a human. It can replicate the patterns of a design system, but it can’t express itself on a page, or clearly articulate designs that meet a user need. Not yet, anyway. It can’t envision bold and brave new ideas. It repeats patterns it’s seen before.

So let it take away the monotony, and let us use our brains for the things that are truly human. That’s how we drive ourselves forward: by focusing on the work that needs thinking. The cool stuff. The exciting stuff.

We’ve been here before

In a way, it’s like design systems. When they came along, there was resistance. People didn’t want every component on a sticker sheet. They wanted to build things by hand.

My answer was no: this removes the painful stuff, the easy stuff, so you can focus on the interesting work. That’s exactly what AI is at present. A multiplier.

From maker to manager

It can do incredible things really quickly. It can get a first pass around 85% of the way there. You’re still the stage gate, doing all the checking and approving and making sure you’re happy. In effect, it makes you a manager of your own output.

We’re moving from creating to curating, and to being something like a reviewer working with a team. You review the output of agents, get them working together and producing things. It’s no different from stepping up into management. We can even get to the point of building whole product and design teams of agents, which is incredible.

It’s also good for spotting opportunities and synthesising data in ways we’d never think of, like pulling user needs together into one solid matrix. But only if we teach it how, and give it the same skills we’d give to people.

Teaching it how we think

In our own heads, we build models of reason. We can still build those, and hand them to AI for the things we find monotonous.

Some work is highly skilled because it’s creative and complex, and that will always need a human. But is some work “highly skilled” only because we’ve built those patterns into our brains to solve problems? If so, can we not hand some of that off?

That’s where this gets a bit philosophical, and I don’t have a tidy answer yet.

And yet

None of this cancels the scepticism. I still don’t know what it all means for the planet, and I don’t think that question should go away. But the tool is here, and it’s at its best when it gives people their time back for the human work.
